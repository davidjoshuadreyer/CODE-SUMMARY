04 · Dislocations and strengthening

Use Burgers vectors, Schmid’s law, and the Hall–Petch relation.

Line defects enable slip

An edge dislocation terminates an extra half-plane of atoms; its Burgers vector is perpendicular to the line. For a screw dislocation, the Burgers vector is parallel to the line. A mixed dislocation has both characters. Motion of dislocations produces permanent slip without requiring an entire plane of bonds to break at once.

Slip systems and repeat distances

A slip system combines a slip plane and a direction within it. FCC commonly slips on {111} in ⟨110⟩ directions. A perfect FCC Burgers vector is (a/2)⟨110⟩ with magnitude a/√2. Distinguish a direction such as [110] from a vector with a physical length.

Resolve the applied stress

Schmid’s law is τ = σ cos φ cos λ. φ is the angle between the loading axis and the slip-plane normal; λ is the angle between the loading axis and slip direction. Slip begins when |τ| reaches the critical resolved shear stress. For a valid slip system the Schmid factor is at most 0.5.

Obstacles increase strength

Cold working increases dislocation density, making dislocations obstruct each other. Solute atoms create strain fields; grain boundaries and particles impede motion. Within the usual Hall–Petch regime, σᵧ = σ₀ + kᵧ/√d. Smaller grains increase the grain-size contribution; keep d consistent with the units of kᵧ.

WORKED EXAMPLE

If σ = 120 MPa, φ = 60° and λ = 30°, τ = 120(0.5)(0.8660) = 51.96 MPa. If the critical resolved shear stress is 60 MPa, this system has not begun to slip. Its required applied stress is 60/(0.5 × 0.8660) = 138.6 MPa.

Check your understanding

If grain diameter falls from 64 μm to 16 μm, how does the Hall–Petch grain-size contribution change?

Show the worked answer

It doubles: (1/√16)/(1/√64) = 2. This doubles σᵧ − σ₀, not necessarily the entire yield strength.
