QUESTION 1 / 6

Density of magnesium oxide

Given / find: One mole of MgO fills a cube 22.37 mm on each side. Find its density in g/cm³.

Why this method: Density means mass per volume. Moles give you the mass; the cube’s side gives you the volume.

  1. Turn one mole into grams

    Add the molar masses of one Mg and one O. Multiplying by one mole leaves a mass in grams.

    M_{\mathrm{MgO}}=24.305+15.999=40.304\ \mathrm{g/mol}

  2. Convert the side before finding the volume

    The requested density uses centimetres, so divide millimetres by 10. Then cube the entire length.

    L=2.237\ \mathrm{cm},\qquad V=L^3=11.1943\ \mathrm{cm^3}

  3. Divide mass by volume

    One mole has mass 40.304 g. Divide by the volume; do not multiply.

    \rho=\frac{m}{V}=\frac{40.304\ \mathrm{g}}{11.1943\ \mathrm{cm^3}}
Answer
\boxed{\rho=3.600\ \mathrm{g/cm^3}}

Check your reasoning: The units are grams per cubic centimetre. Multiplying your answer by 11.1943 cm³ should recover 40.304 g.

Common mistake: Converting volume by a factor of 10. A factor of 10 in length becomes 1000 in volume: 1 cm³ = 1000 mm³.

QUESTION 2 / 6

Size of a cube containing one mole of Mg

Given / find: Find all three dimensions of a solid magnesium cube containing 1 mol. Use molar mass 24.305 g/mol and room-temperature density 1.74 g/cm³.

Why this method: Reverse Question 1: mass and density give volume. The cube root of volume gives the side length.

  1. Supply the material property

    The assignment does not specify magnesium’s density. The Royal Society of Chemistry lists 1.74 g/cm³ and relative atomic mass 24.305. Use your course table’s density if it differs. This assumes a room-temperature bulk sample.

  2. Find the mass and volume

    One mole has mass 24.305 g. Rearrange density = mass/volume to solve for volume.

    V=\frac{m}{\rho}=\frac{24.305}{1.74}=13.9684\ \mathrm{cm^3}

  3. Take the cube root

    All three sides of a cube are equal. The cube root, rather than the square root, reverses cubing.

    L=V^{1/3}=(13.9684)^{1/3}=2.40833\ \mathrm{cm}
Answer
\boxed{2.41\ \mathrm{cm}\times2.41\ \mathrm{cm}\times2.41\ \mathrm{cm}}

Check your reasoning: The side is about 24.1 mm. Cubing the unrounded length and multiplying by the density gives 24.305 g.

Common mistake: Giving 13.97 cm³ as the dimensions. That is the volume; the question asks for three lengths. This is not a unit-cell calculation.

QUESTION 3 / 6

Angle between [110] and [111]

Given / find: Find the angle between these two directions in a cubic crystal.

Why this method: Cubic axes are perpendicular and equally scaled, so the direction indices can be treated as Cartesian vector components. Use the dot product.

  1. Write the two vectors

    Square brackets describe directions. Read each index as a component; [110] has no z component.

    \mathbf{u}=(1,1,0),\qquad\mathbf{v}=(1,1,1)

  2. Calculate the dot product and lengths

    Multiply matching components and add. For each length, square the components, add, and take the square root.

    \mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}=2,\quad|\mathbf{u}|=\sqrt{2},\quad|\mathbf{v}|=\sqrt{3}

  3. Solve for the angle

    Divide the dot product by the product of the lengths. Then use inverse cosine with the calculator in degree mode.

    \cos\theta=\frac{2}{\sqrt{2}\sqrt{3}}=0.816497
Answer
\boxed{\theta=\cos^{-1}(0.816497)=35.26^\circ}

Check your reasoning: [110] is a face diagonal and [111] is a body diagonal. They point into the same region, so their angle should be acute.

Common mistake: Using cosine instead of inverse cosine at the last step, or treating 0.816497 as an angle. The simple index formula relies on the cubic geometry.

QUESTION 4 / 6

Intercepts of the (3 1̄ 1) plane

Given / find: The bar is over the middle 1: h = 3, k = −1, l = 1. Find where the plane crosses the three axes.

Why this method: Miller indices are reciprocals of the intercepts in lattice units. Take reciprocals again to recover the intercepts.

  1. Keep the negative sign

    Read the plane as (3, −1, 1), not (3, 1, 1). Let a, b, c be the lattice lengths along x, y, z.

  2. Take each reciprocal

    Divide the relevant lattice length by its Miller index. The negative index puts the y intercept on the negative y axis.

    x=\frac{a}{h}=\frac{a}{3},\quad y=\frac{b}{k}=-b,\quad z=\frac{c}{l}=c

  3. Label points and sketch the plane

    Draw axes including negative y. Mark (a/3, 0, 0), (0, −b, 0), and (0, 0, c), then join the points. In a cubic lattice, a = b = c.

    \frac{3x}{a}-\frac{y}{b}+\frac{z}{c}=1
Answer
\boxed{x=a/3,\qquad y=-b,\qquad z=c}

Check your reasoning: Substitute each intercept point into the plane equation. Every point gives 1. In lattice units the intercepts are (1/3, −1, 1).

Common mistake: Multiplying all intercepts by 3 to remove the fraction selects a different parallel plane. A zero index would instead mean an infinite intercept: the plane is parallel to that axis.

QUESTION 5 / 6

Vacancy fraction at 660°C

Given / find: At 400°C the fraction is 2.29 × 10⁻⁵. Vacancy formation energy Q = 0.76 eV; k = 8.62 × 10⁻⁵ eV/K. Find the fraction at 660°C.

Why this method: You know the fraction at one temperature. Taking a ratio of the Arrhenius equations eliminates the unknown prefactor A. The Week 2a Bonding slides, slide 36, include this prefactor.

  1. Convert both temperatures to kelvin

    Temperature appears in an exponential energy relation, so Celsius cannot be used. Add 273.15 to each temperature.

    T_1=673.15\ \mathrm{K},\qquad T_2=933.15\ \mathrm{K}

  2. Write the same model at both temperatures

    Assume A and Q stay constant. Divide f₂ by f₁; A cancels. Subtracting the exponents gives the positive temperature-difference expression below.

    f=Ae^{-Q/(kT)}

  3. Rearrange to isolate the new fraction

    The given starting fraction is your anchor. The exponential factor tells you how many times larger it becomes.

    f_2=f_1\exp\!\left[\frac{Q}{k}\left(\frac1{T_1}-\frac1{T_2}\right)\right]

  4. Evaluate the exponent, then multiply

    Q/k = 8816.71 K. The full exponent is 3.64936, so exp(3.64936) = 38.4499. Multiply this by the original fraction.

    f_2=(2.29\times10^{-5})(38.4499)=8.8050\times10^{-4}

  5. Convert a fraction to a percentage

    Multiply by 100. The result is about one vacancy per 1140 lattice sites.

    100f_2=0.08805\%
Answer
\boxed{f_2\approx8.81\times10^{-4}=0.0881\%}

Check your reasoning: The hotter solid has more vacancies: the fraction increases by about 38.45 times. Q and k use matching eV units, so the exponent is dimensionless. To two significant figures, f₂ = 8.8 × 10⁻⁴.

Common mistake: Setting A = 1 and ignoring the starting fraction. That predicts 2.05 × 10⁻⁶ at 400°C, which does not match the given 2.29 × 10⁻⁵. The given data imply A ≈ 11.17; the ratio method uses the lecture model and all supplied data.

QUESTION 6 / 6

How long does carbon diffusion take?

Given / find: Cₛ = 1.0 wt%, C₀ = 0.2 wt%, target C = 0.6 wt%, depth x = 1 mm, D = 2.98 × 10⁻¹¹ m²/s at 1000°C. Find the time.

Why this method: The surface concentration stays fixed while carbon diffuses inward. Use the error-function solution for a semi-infinite solid, with uniform initial concentration, constant temperature and D, and one-dimensional diffusion.

  1. Form the concentration ratio

    Subtract the initial concentration from both the target and the surface value. You can keep all values in wt% because the units cancel.

    \frac{C-C_0}{C_s-C_0}=\frac{0.6-0.2}{1.0-0.2}=0.5

  2. Connect the ratio to the error function

    Define z as depth divided by 2√(Dt). The ratio is 1 − erf(z), so a ratio of 0.5 means erf(z) = 0.5.

    \frac{C-C_0}{C_s-C_0}=1-\operatorname{erf}(z),\quad z=\frac{x}{2\sqrt{Dt}}

  3. Look up and interpolate z

    In a standard error-function table, erf(0.47) = 0.4937 and erf(0.48) = 0.5028. The target 0.5000 lies between them. Interpolate between those two rows; these are not normal-distribution cumulative probabilities.

    z\approx0.47+\frac{0.5000-0.4937}{0.5028-0.4937}(0.01)=0.47692

  4. Rearrange before substituting

    Multiply by 2√(Dt), divide by 2z, square both sides, then divide by D. This is where the factor of 4 and the square on z come from.

    \sqrt{Dt}=\frac{x}{2z}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad t=\frac{x^2}{4Dz^2}

  5. Use metres and calculate seconds

    Convert 1 mm to 0.001 m to match D. The numerator is (10⁻³)² = 10⁻⁶ m².

    t=\frac{(10^{-3})^2}{4(2.98\times10^{-11})(0.47692)^2}\approx36883\ \mathrm{s}

  6. Convert to hours

    Divide seconds by 3600. A more precise inverse error-function value gives 36881 s; the difference is table rounding.

    t\approx\frac{36883}{3600}=10.245\ \mathrm{h}
Answer
\boxed{t\approx3.69\times10^4\ \mathrm{s}\approx10.2\ \mathrm{h}}

Check your reasoning: Substitution gives C = 0.2 + (1.0 − 0.2)(1 − 0.5) = 0.6 wt%. Units are m²/(m²/s) = s. Reaching the same concentration at twice the depth would take four times as long.

Common mistake: Setting z = 0.5. It is erf(z) that equals 0.5. Also keep the factor of 4, square z, and use metres. D is already given at 1000°C, so no further temperature correction is needed.
