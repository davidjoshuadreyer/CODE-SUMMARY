PROBLEM 23.1

Work along a two-dimensional move

Given / find: Fixed q₁ = +2.40 µC at the origin; q₂ = −4.30 µC moves from (0.150, 0) m to (0.250, 0.250) m. Find work done by the electric force.

Why this method: Electric work equals initial minus final potential energy.

  1. Measure distances from the fixed charge

    The final distance is not just the x coordinate. Use Pythagoras from the origin.

    ri=0.150  m,rf=0.2502+0.2502=0.353553  mr_i=0.150\;\mathrm m,\quad r_f=\sqrt{0.250^2+0.250^2}=0.353553\;\mathrm mri​=0.150m,rf​=0.2502+0.2502​=0.353553m

  2. Write initial minus final potential energy

    Use U = kq₁q₂/r. Electric work is W = Ui − Uf. Keep the negative sign of q₂.

    W=kq1q2(1ri−1rf)W=kq_1q_2\left(\frac1{r_i}-\frac1{r_f}\right)W=kq1​q2​(ri​1​−rf​1​)

  3. Substitute in SI units

    µC means 10⁻⁶ C. The distance bracket is 6.6667 − 2.8284 = 3.8382 m⁻¹.

    W=(8.988×109)(2.40×10−6)(−4.30×10−6)(3.8382)=−0.356  JW=(8.988\times10^9)(2.40\times10^{-6})(-4.30\times10^{-6})(3.8382)=-0.356\;\mathrm JW=(8.988×109)(2.40×10−6)(−4.30×10−6)(3.8382)=−0.356J
Answer

−0.356 J.

Check your reasoning: Moving unlike charges apart raises potential energy; electric work is negative.

Common mistake: Dropping charge signs, or calculating work by an external agent instead of work by the electric force.

PROBLEM 23.5

Closest approach of repelling charges

Given / find: Fixed q₁ = −2.80 µC; moving q₂ = −7.80 µC, m = 1.50 g, initially 0.800 m away at 22.0 m/s toward q₁. Find speed at 0.400 m and minimum separation.

Why this method: Repulsion converts kinetic energy into electric potential energy.

  1. Find the constant product kq₁q₂

    Both charges are negative, so their product and potential energy are positive.

    A=kq1q2=0.196298  J mA=kq_1q_2=0.196298\;\mathrm{J\,m}A=kq1​q2​=0.196298Jm

  2. Find the total starting energy

    Convert 1.50 g to 0.00150 kg. Add kinetic and electric potential energy.

    E=12(0.00150)(22.0)2+0.1962980.800=0.608372  JE=\tfrac12(0.00150)(22.0)^2+\frac{0.196298}{0.800}=0.608372\;\mathrm JE=21​(0.00150)(22.0)2+0.8000.196298​=0.608372J

  3. Find kinetic energy at 0.400 m

    Potential energy increases as the repelling charges get closer. Subtract it from the same total energy.

    K=0.608372−0.1962980.400=0.117628  JK=0.608372-\frac{0.196298}{0.400}=0.117628\;\mathrm JK=0.608372−0.4000.196298​=0.117628J

  4. Convert kinetic energy to speed

    Rearrange K = ½mv².

    v=2Km=12.5  m/sv=\sqrt{\frac{2K}{m}}=12.5\;\mathrm{m/s}v=m2K​​=12.5m/s

  5. Set kinetic energy to zero at the turning point

    The moving sphere stops momentarily at minimum separation. All the mechanical energy is then electric potential energy.

    rmin=AE=0.323  mr_{min}=\frac{A}{E}=0.323\;\mathrm mrmin​=EA​=0.323m
Answer

12.5 m/s; minimum separation 0.323 m.

Check your reasoning: The turning point is closer than 0.400 m, so the intermediate speed is real and smaller than 22 m/s.

Common mistake: Setting potential energy to zero at closest approach. It is kinetic energy that vanishes there.

PROBLEM 23.7

Two protons approaching head-on

Given / find: Two protons start far apart, each moving at 2.00 × 10⁵ m/s toward the other in the Earth frame; mₚ = 1.673 × 10⁻²⁷ kg. Find their maximum repulsive force.

Why this method: Both protons contribute kinetic energy, and both stop momentarily at closest approach.

  1. Include both moving protons

    Their equal and opposite momenta make the centre of mass stationary. Both stop momentarily at closest approach. Initial potential energy at several metres is negligible.

    Ki=2(12mpv2)=mpv2=6.692×10−17  JK_i=2(\tfrac12 m_pv^2)=m_pv^2=6.692\times10^{-17}\;\mathrm JKi​=2(21​mp​v2)=mp​v2=6.692×10−17J

  2. Turn all initial kinetic energy into pair energy

    There is one interacting pair. Set k e²/rmin equal to the total kinetic energy.

    rmin=ke2Ki=3.45×10−12  mr_{min}=\frac{ke^2}{K_i}=3.45\times10^{-12}\;\mathrm mrmin​=Ki​ke2​=3.45×10−12m

  3. Use Coulomb’s law at the smallest separation

    Force is largest when r is smallest. Use the unrounded separation.

    Fmax=ke2rmin2=1.94×10−5  NF_{max}=\frac{ke^2}{r_{min}^2}=1.94\times10^{-5}\;\mathrm NFmax​=rmin2​ke2​=1.94×10−5N
Answer

Maximum force 1.94 × 10⁻⁵ N.

Check your reasoning: Using only one proton’s kinetic energy would give twice the separation and one-quarter the force.

Common mistake: Using only one proton’s kinetic energy, or putting the relative speed 4.00 × 10⁵ m/s into the kinetic energy of each proton.

PROBLEM 23.8

Energy of three equal charges

Given / find: Three charges q = +1.20 µC occupy an equilateral triangle of side a = 0.400 m. Find the energy relative to infinite separation.

Why this method: Count distinct pairs once: there are three.

  1. List each unique pair

    The three pairs are (1,2), (1,3), and (2,3). Every distance is 0.400 m. Do not count both (1,2) and (2,1).

  2. Find energy per pair

    Use q = 1.20 × 10⁻⁶ C and U_pair = kq²/a.

    Upair=(8.988×109)(1.20×10−6)20.400=0.0323568  JU_{pair}=\frac{(8.988\times10^9)(1.20\times10^{-6})^2}{0.400}=0.0323568\;\mathrm JUpair​=0.400(8.988×109)(1.20×10−6)2​=0.0323568J

  3. Add three identical contributions

    All charges have the same sign, so every contribution is positive.

    U=3Upair=0.0970704  JU=3U_{pair}=0.0970704\;\mathrm JU=3Upair​=0.0970704J
Answer

0.0971 J.

Check your reasoning: The energy is positive: external work is required to assemble repelling charges.

Common mistake: Double-counting the pairs. The system has three pair energies, not six.

PROBLEM 23.19

Potentials at two points

Given / find: q₁ = +2.40 nC and q₂ = −6.50 nC are 0.100 m apart. A is their midpoint; B is 0.0800 m from q₁ and 0.0600 m from q₂. Find Vₐ, Vᵦ, and electric work moving +2.50 nC from B to A.

Why this method: Potential is a signed scalar sum; work uses start minus finish.

  1. Find the potential at A

    A is halfway along the 0.100 m separation, so it is 0.0500 m from each charge. Potential adds as signed scalars, without vector components.

    VA=k(2.40×10−90.0500−6.50×10−90.0500)=−737.016  VV_A=k\left(\frac{2.40\times10^{-9}}{0.0500}-\frac{6.50\times10^{-9}}{0.0500}\right)=-737.016\;\mathrm VVA​=k(0.05002.40×10−9​−0.05006.50×10−9​)=−737.016V

  2. Find the potential at B

    Use the different stated distances: 0.0800 m to q₁ and 0.0600 m to q₂.

    VB=k(2.40×10−90.0800−6.50×10−90.0600)=−704.060  VV_B=k\left(\frac{2.40\times10^{-9}}{0.0800}-\frac{6.50\times10^{-9}}{0.0600}\right)=-704.060\;\mathrm VVB​=k(0.08002.40×10−9​−0.06006.50×10−9​)=−704.060V

  3. Take starting minus ending potential

    The test charge goes from B to A, so use VB − VA. Multiply by +2.50 nC.

    WB→A=(2.50×10−9)(−704.060+737.016)=8.24×10−8  JW_{B\to A}=(2.50\times10^{-9})(-704.060+737.016)=8.24\times10^{-8}\;\mathrm JWB→A​=(2.50×10−9)(−704.060+737.016)=8.24×10−8J
Answer

Vₐ = −737 V; Vᵦ = −704 V; W = +8.24 × 10⁻⁸ J.

Check your reasoning: The positive test charge moves to lower potential, so the field does positive work.

Common mistake: Reversing VB − VA or rounding both potentials too early. Keep guard digits in the small difference.

PROBLEM 23.27

Electron on a charged ring axis

Given / find: Ring radius R = 0.150 m, charge Q = +24.0 nC. An electron starts at rest on its axis, x₀ = 0.300 m; constrain motion to the axis. Describe motion and find the speed through the centre.

Why this method: The attractive force restores the electron toward the ring plane.

  1. Write the ring’s on-axis potential

    Every small ring element is the same distance √(R² + x²) from a point on the axis. Add their scalar potentials.

    V(x)=kQR2+x2V(x)=\frac{kQ}{\sqrt{R^2+x^2}}V(x)=R2+x2​kQ​

  2. Compare the release point with the centre

    Use R = 0.150 m, x₀ = 0.300 m and Q = 24.0 × 10⁻⁹ C.

    V(0)=1438.08  V,V(x0)≈643.13  VV(0)=1438.08\;\mathrm V,\quad V(x_0)\approx643.13\;\mathrm VV(0)=1438.08V,V(x0​)≈643.13V

  3. Use the electron’s negative charge

    U = −eV decreases as V increases. Starting from rest, the lost potential energy becomes kinetic energy.

    12mev2=e[V(0)−V(x0)]\tfrac12m_ev^2=e[V(0)-V(x_0)]21​me​v2=e[V(0)−V(x0​)]

  4. Solve for the centre speed

    The potential rise is about 794.95 V.

    v=2(1.602×10−19)(794.95)9.109×10−31=1.67×107  m/sv=\sqrt{\frac{2(1.602\times10^{-19})(794.95)}{9.109\times10^{-31}}}=1.67\times10^7\;\mathrm{m/s}v=9.109×10−312(1.602×10−19)(794.95)​​=1.67×107m/s

  5. Describe what happens after crossing the centre

    The force is zero exactly at the centre, but the electron has its maximum speed there. It continues through, slows down, stops at x = −0.300 m, and returns. Neglecting radiation and losses, it oscillates between ±0.300 m. This amplitude is not in the small-displacement simple-harmonic regime.

Answer

Oscillatory motion; centre speed 1.67 × 10⁷ m/s.

Check your reasoning: It oscillates between ±0.300 m, fastest at the centre. This finite-amplitude motion is not simple harmonic because force is not proportional to x.

Common mistake: Thinking zero force at the centre means zero speed. The speed is greatest there; inertia carries the electron across.
