Impulse inputs and the Dirac delta

Model a short kick and understand the jump it causes.

Before you start: Laplace transforms and time shifts.

THE IDEA

Understand before calculating

The delta represents concentrated area

The Dirac delta is an idealized impulse rather than an ordinary finite-valued function. An impulse Jδ(t−a)J\delta(t-a)Jδ(t−a) has total strength JJJ. Its transform is L{δ(t−a)}=e−as,a>0.\mathcal L\{\delta(t-a)\}=e^{-as},\qquad a>0.L{δ(t−a)}=e−as,a>0.

What jumps?

For my′′+cy′+ky=Jδ(t−a)my''+cy'+ky=J\delta(t-a)my′′+cy′+ky=Jδ(t−a), displacement remains continuous and velocity jumps by y′(a+)−y′(a−)=Jm.y'(a^+)-y'(a^-)=\frac Jm.y′(a+)−y′(a−)=mJ​. Integrate the equation across a tiny interval around aaa to obtain this jump. In a first-order equation y′+ky=Jδ(t−a)y'+ky=J\delta(t-a)y′+ky=Jδ(t−a), it is yyy itself that jumps by JJJ.

Your method checklist

  1. Transform the delta into its exponential delay factor.
  2. Solve for the transformed response.
  3. Invert the undelayed part, then delay it.
  4. Check behaviour before the impulse and the correct jump size.
